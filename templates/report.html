Final Analysis & Findings

Case Study 135: Personal Finance Classification Using Machine Learning B.Tech Computer Science & Engineering (2024-28) — Semester V Machine Learning

📌 1. Executive Summary

This case study addresses the challenge of automatically profiling and classifying individual financial behavior into actionable archetypes (Saver, Balanced, and High-Spender) using machine learning techniques. Leveraging a dataset of 3,600 multi-dimensional financial profiles, we performed domain-specific feature engineering, exploratory data analysis, and benchmarked six supervised classification algorithms: Logistic Regression, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Decision Tree, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and Support Vector Machine (SVM).

Key Benchmark Result

The K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) and Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifiers achieved the top test accuracy of 97.36% and macro F1-score of 97.42%, closely followed by Logistic Regression (97.22%) and Random Forest (97.08%).

🔍 2. Important Financial Behavior Patterns

Through systematic exploratory analysis, several prominent financial behavior patterns were identified:

  • The Savings-to-Income Divergence: Savers maintain an average savings rate of 36.08% and an expense-to-income ratio of 43.22%. In contrast, High-Spenders exhibit an average savings rate of just 5.35% with expenses consuming 96.33% of monthly earnings.
  • Discretionary Spending as the Core Risk Driver: While essential expenditures (housing, groceries, utilities) remain relatively stable as a percentage of essential living across all income brackets, High-Spenders allocate 39.56% of their total expenditures to discretionary wants (entertainment, nightlife, personal luxury), compared to only 11.31% for Savers and 22.72% for Balanced individuals.
  • Debt-to-Income (DTI) & Credit Card Trap: High-Spenders carry an average credit card utilization of 72.2% and a DTI ratio of 29.75%, creating compounding interest drag that severely impedes capital accumulation.
  • Behavior Over Income Volume: High earning power does not guarantee Saver status. Across the dataset, individuals earning >$12,000/month frequently fell into the High-Spender archetype due to lifestyle inflation, reinforcing that financial health is fundamentally a behavioral discipline rather than purely an income metric.

🏆 3. Best Classification Model & Technical Justification

Based on multi-metric evaluation across 5-fold cross validation and held-out test data (N = 720):

  • Top Empirical Accuracy: K-Nearest Neighbors (k=7, distance-weighted) achieved 97.36% test accuracy, 97.42% macro F1, and a 5-fold CV accuracy of 96.63% ± 0.77%.
  • Why Distance & Kernel Methods Excelled: Once continuous features were standardized into normalized ratio space (Savings Rate, Expense Ratio, DTI, Discretionary Ratio), the archetypes naturally separated into distinct high-density clusters in Euclidean space, allowing KNN and RBF-kernel SVM to accurately draw tight, non-linear decision boundaries around boundary cases.
  • Industry Deployment Trade-Off: While KNN and SVM achieved peak statistical metrics, Logistic Regression (97.22%) and Random Forest (97.08%) offer superior computational efficiency (O(1) inference time vs. O(N) distance lookups for KNN) and explainable feature coefficients/importances, making them highly attractive for production fintech microservices.

📊 4. Classification Accuracy & Error Analysis

Detailed analysis of confusion matrices and per-class metrics reveals:

  • High Recall on Extreme Classes: All models achieved over 98.5% precision and recall on the Saver class, as high savings rates and low expense ratios provide an unambiguous mathematical boundary.
  • Boundary Ambiguity: Approximately 2.5% of test samples were misclassified, exclusively between Balanced and High-Spender. These correspond to borderline edge cases (e.g., users with 78% expense ratios and moderate debt) where behavioral transition is naturally fuzzy.

⚠️ 5. Real-World Limitations

  • Cross-Sectional vs. Longitudinal Data: The current system evaluates a static monthly snapshot. Real-world personal finance is dynamic; single-month anomalies (e.g., annual tuition payments, wedding expenses, or unexpected medical emergencies) could misclassify a disciplined Saver as a High-Spender without multi-month smoothing.
  • Cost of Living (COL) Variance: Rent and cost of living vary drastically between tier-1 metropolitan cities and regional areas. A higher housing-to-income ratio in San Francisco or Mumbai does not necessarily signify reckless spending.
  • Reliance on Self-Reported Metrics: When deployed via manual input forms, cognitive bias and social desirability bias often lead users to under-report impulse shopping and overestimate savings.

🚀 6. Potential FinTech Applications

  • Automated Robo-Advisory: Integration into wealth platforms to tailor asset allocations—recommending aggressive index investing for Savers and automated cash-flow budgeting for High-Spenders.
  • Early Delinquency Warning for Banks: Commercial banks can deploy this model on transactional data to detect transitions from Balanced to High-Spender months before credit card defaults occur.
  • AI-Driven Personal Financial Management (PFM) Apps: Mobile apps (e.g., Mint, YNAB, CRED) can deliver contextual micro-nudges when discretionary ratios exceed safe thresholds during mid-month spending.
  • Alternative Credit Underwriting: Providing non-traditional credit scores for gig-economy workers or young professionals lacking formal credit histories by demonstrating disciplined saving habits.
