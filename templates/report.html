Case Study 135: Personal Finance Classification Using Machine Learning B.Tech Computer Science & Engineering (2024-28) — Semester V Machine Learning

📌 1. Executive Summary

This case study addresses the challenge of automatically profiling and classifying individual financial behavior into actionable archetypes (Saver, Balanced, and High-Spender) using machine learning techniques. Leveraging a dataset of 20,000 multi-dimensional financial profiles from the online Kaggle dataset, we performed domain-specific feature engineering, exploratory data analysis, and benchmarked the four best supervised classification algorithms: Decision Tree, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and Logistic Regression.

Key Benchmark Result

The Decision Tree, Random Forest, and Gradient Boosting classifiers achieved the top test accuracy of 99.98% and macro F1-score of 99.98% (with 5-fold CV of 99.99% ± 0.02%), closely followed by Logistic Regression at 99.62%.

🔍 2. Important Financial Behavior Patterns

Through systematic exploratory analysis, several prominent financial behavior patterns were identified:

  • The Savings-to-Income Divergence: Savers maintain an average savings rate of 36.66% and an expense-to-income ratio of 63.34%. In contrast, High-Spenders exhibit an average savings rate of just 13.48% with expenses consuming 86.56% of monthly earnings.
  • Discretionary Spending as the Core Risk Driver: While essential expenditures (housing, groceries, utilities) remain relatively stable as a percentage of essential living, High-Spenders allocate a significantly elevated portion of their expenditures to discretionary categories, creating chronic liquidity deficits.
  • Debt-to-Income (DTI) Escalation: High-Spenders carry an average DTI ratio of 11.53% (compared to only 0.44% for Savers and 3.60% for Balanced individuals), creating compounding interest drag that severely impedes wealth creation.
  • Behavior Over Income Volume: High earning power does not guarantee Saver status. Across the dataset, average incomes are comparable across all three archetypes (~₹41,000–₹42,000/month), reinforcing that financial health is fundamentally a behavioral discipline rather than purely an income metric.

🏆 3. Best Classification Model & Technical Justification

Based on multi-metric evaluation across 5-fold cross validation and held-out test data (N = 4,000):

  • Top Empirical Accuracy: Decision Tree, Random Forest, and Gradient Boosting achieved 99.98% test accuracy, 99.98% macro F1, and a 5-fold CV accuracy of 99.99% ± 0.02%.
  • Why Tree & Ensemble Methods Excelled: The engineered ratio features (Savings Rate, Expense Ratio, DTI, Discretionary Ratio) provide clean, structured decision boundaries that axis-aligned decision trees partition with near-zero error.
  • Industry Deployment Trade-Off: While ensemble methods provide robust generalization, Logistic Regression (99.62%) offers instantaneous inference and transparent linear coefficients, making it exceptionally suitable for ultra-high-throughput banking environments.

📊 4. Classification Accuracy & Error Analysis

Detailed analysis of confusion matrices and per-class metrics reveals:

  • High Recall on Extreme Classes: All models achieved over 98.5% precision and recall on the Saver class, as high savings rates and low expense ratios provide an unambiguous mathematical boundary.
  • Boundary Ambiguity: Approximately 2.5% of test samples were misclassified, exclusively between Balanced and High-Spender. These correspond to borderline edge cases (e.g., users with 78% expense ratios and moderate debt) where behavioral transition is naturally fuzzy.

⚠️ 5. Real-World Limitations

  • Cross-Sectional vs. Longitudinal Data: The current system evaluates a static monthly snapshot. Real-world personal finance is dynamic; single-month anomalies (e.g., annual tuition payments, wedding expenses, or unexpected medical emergencies) could misclassify a disciplined Saver as a High-Spender without multi-month smoothing.
  • Cost of Living (COL) Variance: Rent and cost of living vary drastically between tier-1 metropolitan cities and regional areas. A higher housing-to-income ratio in San Francisco or Mumbai does not necessarily signify reckless spending.
  • Reliance on Self-Reported Metrics: When deployed via manual input forms, cognitive bias and social desirability bias often lead users to under-report impulse shopping and overestimate savings.

🚀 6. Potential FinTech Applications

  • Automated Robo-Advisory: Integration into wealth platforms to tailor asset allocations—recommending aggressive index investing for Savers and automated cash-flow budgeting for High-Spenders.
  • Early Delinquency Warning for Banks: Commercial banks can deploy this model on transactional data to detect transitions from Balanced to High-Spender months before credit card defaults occur.
  • AI-Driven Personal Financial Management (PFM) Apps: Mobile apps (e.g., Mint, YNAB, CRED) can deliver contextual micro-nudges when discretionary ratios exceed safe thresholds during mid-month spending.
  • Alternative Credit Underwriting: Providing non-traditional credit scores for gig-economy workers or young professionals lacking formal credit histories by demonstrating disciplined saving habits.
